# Storytelling Guide: Performing Comics, Webtoons and Picture Books

The method the narration system uses to write scripts. Load this file in the writing (stage 9), editing (stage 10) and QA (stage 11) stages of `narration-pipeline.md`.

**Audience:** 13–20, leaning older.
**Goal:** one seamless, cinematic performance. The listener never hears the mechanics of the page, only the story.

---

## Core principle: one seamless story

The art, speech bubbles, captions and sound effects are raw material blended into a single flowing story. The listener should never be able to tell where narration ends and dialogue begins, and should never hear the words panel, bubble, caption, page or layout.

**Division of labor:**
- **Page text is fixed.** Dialogue, thoughts and captions are spoken exactly as written.
- **The narration around it is yours.** Action, bridges between images, atmosphere and pacing.

---

## 1. Studying the story first

Before writing a word, go through the whole storyboard.

- **Map the arc.** Setup, the inciting moment, three to five rising beats, climax, resolution. Know how much runway you have before the climax.
- **Write the one-sentence engine.** "A [who] must [what] or [stakes]." It shapes the opening and the pace.
- **Mark protected reveals.** Twists, secret identities and hidden abilities. Note where each may be quietly set up and where it lands. Never hint early.
- **Chart the emotional peaks.** The loudest moment, the quietest, the longest pause. Two identical peaks in a row need different delivery.
- **Find the callbacks.** Anything that returns later gets a small verbal marker the first time, so the payoff clicks.
- **Know your cast.** Every speaker has a label and voice in the character bible before drafting.

**Example**
> *Arc note:* Setup: a fighter disguised in a festival crowd. Rising: the "performers" turn out to be enemies; he can't use his hidden power in public. Climax: he finds a way to use it unseen. Resolution: an observer realizes who he is.
> *Setup/payoff:* He starts to say a power's name mid-leap and stops himself. Later, he says it in full, quietly. Perform the first as a bitten-off half-word so the second lands.

## 2. Reading an image

**What to look for in every image**
- **Faces:** eyebrows, mouth corners, where the eyes point. A glance off-frame often means someone is arriving.
- **Body:** weight, hands, distance between characters. Leaning in reads as intimacy or threat; leaning away as fear or disgust.
- **Color and light:** warm or cold, the light source, sudden washes (a red flood is an emotional cue).
- **Composition:** who is large, who is small, low angles (power), high angles (vulnerability).
- **Motion lines and blur:** direction, speed, impact.
- **Background details:** objects and signs that may matter later.
- **What changed since the last image.** The story lives in the difference.

**Turning visuals into narration**
- Describe the one or two details that carry meaning, not everything visible.
- Convert pictures into verbs. "A girl stands on a roof, wind in her hair" becomes *"Wind shoves at her from the harbor side, and she leans into it."*
- Color becomes mood, not a color report. Say *"The sky has gone the color of a bruise,"* not "The sky is purple."

**Bridging the gaps**
- **Add the motion between images.** Door in one image, stairs in the next: *"She's through the door and taking the stairs two at a time."*
- **Carry a sound across the cut.** A noise that starts in one image and ends in the next stitches them together.
- **Use time phrases, never layout phrases.** "By the time she reaches the top..." Never "In the next picture..."

**Example**
> *Image A:* A boy grips a railing, knuckles white, a storm cloud behind him. *Image B:* The same railing, empty; one shoe on the deck.
> *"Rafi holds that railing like it's the only solid thing left in the world. The cloud behind him swells, black and heavy (short pause) and then the railing is empty. Just one shoe, on its side on the wet deck."*

## 3. Weaving text into the story

### Reading the page correctly

- **Speaker:** follow the bubble's tail to a mouth. A tail pointing off-frame is a voice from outside the scene. Joined bubbles are one speaker, one breath with a pause.
- **Order:** follow the reading format set in the pipeline (stage 4). In vertical-scroll webtoons, higher on the screen means earlier in time.
- **Trust the storyboard.** Speaker, type and order come from the verified storyboard. If a line is marked `judgment_call` or `majority`, perform it, but don't build a major beat on an uncertain attribution.

### Bubble types and how to perform them

| Type | How it's performed |
|---|---|
| Speech | The character's normal voice |
| Shout | Loud, sharp attack, often shorter |
| Whisper | Drop the volume, add breath, lean close |
| Thought | Softer, dry, intimate; other characters don't react |
| Caption | Your narrator voice, fully blended |
| Wavy or wobbly text | Unsteady rhythm (weak, sick, dazed) |
| Bold or oversized word | Hit that word, not the whole line |
| Trailing "..." | Let it hang; never finish it for them |
| Sound effect | Performed as a sound or folded into the action |

**Never announce a type.** "She shouts in a big jagged bubble" is always wrong.

### Weaving techniques

**Action beats.** Attach the line to what the body is doing.
> Instead of: *"Mia says, 'Get down!'"*
> Perform: *Mia throws herself across the floor. "Get down!"*

**Emotional lead-ins.** Let the face set up the delivery.
> *Grandpa doesn't look up. His jaw works for a second before anything comes out. (quiet) "It was my fault."*

**Captions become narration.** A caption is spoken in your voice, with no introduction.
> Wrong: *"The box at the top says, 'That night, the city flooded.'"*
> Right: *"That night, the city flooded."*

**Sound effects become events.** Perform the big ones; fold in the small ones.
> Instead of: *"There's a sound effect that says CRASH."*
> Perform: *CRASH. The window explodes inward, glass glittering across the floor.*
> Folded: *The door slams hard enough to rattle the pictures on the wall.*

**Non-English sound effects.** Korean, Japanese or Chinese lettering drawn into the art is never read, romanized or translated aloud. Perform the sound it represents (a whoosh, a boom, a crackle), or narrate the action it marks.
> *Lettering in the art:* a large Korean sound effect across a lion costume slamming into brick.
> *Perform:* *"The head comes down like a falling boulder, and the brick explodes where he was standing a heartbeat ago."*

**Growls and repeated noises.** Perform one clearly, then narrate the rest.
> Four snarling "KRRK!" lines become *"They snarl from every side at once. 'Krrk! Krrrk!'"*

**Free indirect narration.** Let the narration take on the attitude of the character in focus.
> *"Of course the wolf picked now to go belly-up and beg. Of course."*

**Minimal dialogue tags.** Name a speaker the first time they appear in a scene, or after a long silence. After that, voice, action beats and context carry it.

**Smooth transitions.**
- Narration into dialogue: end on the character's body or face, then speak the line.
- Dialogue into narration: follow the line with its consequence.
> *"'Don't open it.' (beat) She opens it."*

**Rapid exchanges and crowded panels.**
- **Anchor once, then ping-pong.** Tag both speakers on the first exchange, then alternate voices without tags.
- **Stagger crowds.** Perform two or three lines distinctly and let the rest become noise. Any line that carries plot or character must still be spoken verbatim.

**Keep the exact words.** Stutters, ellipses and punctuation are part of the line. Add breath, pauses and stress, never words.

### Wrong vs. right

**1. The ambush**
*Page:* A thief lunges from behind crates, knife raised. Shout: "YOUR BAG. NOW." Small whisper from the victim: "okay… okay." Sound effect: a blade being drawn.
> **Separate:** *"This page shows a thief jumping out. There's a sound effect. He shouts in a spiky bubble, 'Your bag, now.' Then the girl says in a small bubble, 'Okay, okay.'"*
> **Woven:** *Steel slides out of a sleeve with a thin metallic hiss, and a man comes up from behind the crates fast. (loud, hard) "YOUR BAG. NOW." Juno's hands are already up, her voice barely there. (whisper) "okay… okay."*

**2. The quiet reveal**
*Page:* Caption: "The house had been empty for years." A dusty kitchen, one teacup steaming. Thought from Leo: "Someone was just here."
> **Separate:** *"The caption says the house had been empty for years. There's a teacup. Leo thinks, 'Someone was just here.'"*
> **Woven:** *"The house had been empty for years. Dust sits on the counters thick as felt, on the chairs, on the stopped clock. And in the middle of the table, a teacup (slow) still steaming. (inner voice, close and dry) Someone was just here."*

**3. Rapid back-and-forth**
*Page:* Ada: "You said you locked it." Ben: "I did lock it." Ada, one word bold: "Then **why** is it open?" Ben, trailing: "I…"
> **Separate:** *"Ada says, 'You said you locked it.' Ben says, 'I did lock it.' Ada says, 'Then why is it open?' Ben says, 'I…'"*
> **Woven:** *Ada spins around, the gate swinging behind her. (sharp) "You said you locked it." (Ben: lower, defensive) "I did lock it." "Then* why *is it open?" Ben opens his mouth, and nothing comes. (trailing off) "I…"*

## 4. Telling characters apart

**Voice dials.** Set each character on five dials, and store them in the character bible:
- **Pitch:** low, mid, high. Vary within genders; don't default to "girls high, boys low."
- **Pace:** rushed, even, deliberate.
- **Rhythm:** clipped fragments, flowing sentences, a signature pause.
- **Texture:** breathy, gravelly, nasal, bright, flat.
- **Attitude:** the one emotion under everything they say.

**Consistency**
- **Hold the settings for the whole story.** When a character grows, change one dial, so they're still recognizable.
- **No heavy or stereotyped accents.** Rhythm and attitude do the work.

**Clarity test.** Read a two-character exchange with every tag removed. If it's unclear who's talking, widen the gap between the two voices on at least two dials.

**Labels and names**
- **Unnamed characters** get a descriptive label ("the watcher," "the masked woman").
- **When a name is revealed,** bridge the label to the name once: *"The watcher, the one they call the Cursed Killing Star..."*
- **Vary repeated labels** after the first appearance: a pronoun, or a secondary label like "the spy."
- **Never use a name the pages haven't given.**

**Example**
> *Bible:* Captain Orr: low, slow, gravelly, contempt; stress lands on the last word. Pip: high-mid, fast, bright, eager; sentences run together.
> *(Orr) "You'll scrub the deck until it* shines*." (Pip, tumbling) "Yes-sir-right-away-sir." Orr doesn't even look at him.*

## 5. Building the world

- **One sense per new location.** Give each place one strong non-visual detail implied by the art: steam means heat and hiss, rain means drumming.
- **Establish, then trust.** Paint a place fully once, then refer back to it in a phrase.
- **Match the detail to the art style.** Loose, cartoony art gets light description; painted, moody art earns lusher language.
- **Recurring textures** become the world's soundtrack: "the hum of the tower," "the salt wind."
- **Additions are texture only.** Sound, smell and temperature that fit the art are allowed. New objects, events, characters or lore are not. Never contradict the art: if it shows noon, it's not moonlight.

**Example**
> *Art:* A night market under paper lanterns, crowds, smoke rising from grills.
> *"The night market smells like charcoal and sugar. Lanterns bob overhead on their strings, throwing orange light across a hundred faces, and somewhere a vendor is shouting prices nobody is listening to."*

## 6. Pacing, tension and the climax

**Speed**
- **Slow down** for reveals, grief, awe, the beat before danger, and first sights. Use longer sentences, lower pitch and pauses.
- **Speed up** for chases, fights, arguments and panic. Use short fragments and fewer pauses. Sentence length should match the pulse of the scene.
- **Collapse repetition.** Three identical hits become one line with a rhythm: *"Hit. Hit. Hit. And he's still standing."*

**Silence**
- **One beat:** before a punchline or reversal.
- **Two beats:** before a reveal or a devastating line.
- **Long pause:** after a cliffhanger, at a scroll break or page turn, and after a title card.

**Scroll breaks and page turns**
- In picture books and print comics, the page turn is the cliffhanger point.
- In vertical webtoons, the equivalent is a stretch of empty scroll or a big black or white gap. Treat it as a held pause.
- End each section on its most open element: an unanswered line, a reaching hand, a sound with no source.

**Building to the climax**
- **Escalate one variable per beat:** volume, speed or stakes. Save all three for the climax.
- **Save the peak voice.** If you shout early, the climax has nowhere to go.
- **Pay off the callbacks at the climax.**

**Landing the ending**
- Slow down for the final section.
- End on a concrete image or a gut-punch line, then silence. No summary, no outro.

**Example**
> *Final image:* The boy lets the paper boat go; caption: "He didn't watch it go."
> *(slow) "He sets the boat on the water and lets go. The current takes it gently at first, then faster. (pause) He didn't watch it go." (silence)*

## 7. Pitching it for 13–20, leaning older

- **Talk across, not down.** Assume a smart, slightly impatient listener. Cut explanations the art already makes obvious.
- **Humor lives in timing and attitude.** Deadpan reversals, dry understatement, the narrator's opinion of a character. Never jokes addressed to the listener.
- **Narrator slang: light touch.** A sharp modern phrase can add flavor (one per scene at most), and it must never replace page text. It shouldn't clash with the story's setting in a serious beat.
- **Take the stakes seriously.** Fear, betrayal, loss and menace get room to land. Don't undercut a heavy moment with a quick joke.
- **Cool, not cute.** No sing-song intonation or over-bright voices. Restraint and contrast read as confidence.
- **Page language stays verbatim.** Profanity, violence and menace on the page are performed as written. The narrator's own additions stay clean and never sexualize characters.
- **Jargon on first appearance.** An in-world term may get a half-line of context the first time, but only as far as the art and text support: *"Gingong, and his body goes light as smoke."* Never define lore the pages don't define. A name spoken with weight is often scarier than an explanation.

**Example**
> *Page:* A small dog chases off a huge bear. Dog: "And STAY out."
> *Storytime version:* *"Oh wow! Look at that brave little doggy! Yay!"*
> *This audience:* *"The bear is roughly the size of a car. The dog is roughly the size of a boot. The bear leaves anyway. (tiny, furious) 'And STAY out.'"*

## 8. Wordless and difficult pages

**Wordless images**
- Narrate what changes, and let the silence become the mood.
- A slow-motion sequence becomes short, spaced sentences with pauses.
- Show emotion through the body, not labels: "Her shoulders fold in," not "She is sad."

**Ambiguous images**
- Choose the reading that fits the story so far and the next beat.
- If it's genuinely unclear, narrate what's certain and leave the rest open: *"Something moves in the reeds. It might be the wind."*

**Busy action**
- Find the spine: the one action the image is about. Tell that first, then one or two supporting details.
- Order the chaos by cause and effect, not by position on the page.

**Tricky layouts**
- **Splash images:** slow down, move from the most important detail outward, and hold a beat of silence.
- **Two angles of one action:** narrate them as one moment.
- **Inset close-ups:** treat them as the camera zooming in: *"And up close, you can see her hand is shaking."*
- **Flashbacks** (sepia, soft edges, desaturated): shift to a softer, past-tense narrator, and return with a firm present-tense anchor.

**Non-story material**
- **Credits, watermarks, scan-site banners and promo panels** are never narrated.
- **A series title card mid-chapter** is a pause, not text.

**Uncertain lines**
- Perform only lines the storyboard marks as usable.
- An omitted `unresolved` line is bridged by narration of the action alone. Never invent words to fill the gap, and never paraphrase the missing line.

**Example**
> *Wordless:* A city at dawn, one tiny figure on a rooftop in the corner.
> *(slow) "Dawn comes up over the drowned city, gold on the water, gold on a thousand broken windows. And way up on one rooftop, so small you'd miss her, Tam is watching all of it."*

---

## 9. Rules you never break

1. **Never contradict the art or the dialogue.** If it's shown, it's true.
2. **Never invent major plot events, characters, objects or lore.** Additions are texture only.
3. **Never change page text.** Dialogue, thoughts and captions are verbatim, or omitted if unresolved. Never paraphrase.
4. **Never mention panels, bubbles, captions, pages or layout.** That includes "the scene shifts," "cut to" and "next image."
5. **Never read text as a separate item.** Everything happens inside the story.
6. **Never read, romanize or translate non-English sound-effect lettering.** Perform the sound.
7. **Never narrate credits, watermarks or promo panels.**
8. **Never ask the audience questions or invite participation.** A question needed for suspense belongs to a character.
9. **Never break immersion.** No asides to the listener, no meta commentary.
10. **Never let a character's voice slip.** If it does, correct it on the next line without comment.
11. **Never spoil a protected reveal.**
12. **Never use a name the pages haven't given, and never let a name drift.** One pronunciation per character.
13. **Never repeat a tag or label so often it becomes noise.**
14. **Never undercut real emotion with a reflexive joke.**
15. **Never add sexualized, cruel or demeaning commentary of your own.**
16. **Never perform an unverified line as if it were confirmed.**

## Worked example: *The Letter-Runner*

### Source (three pages)

**Page 1**
- *Image 1:* A flooded city at dusk; rooftops linked by rope bridges. Tam (about 13, oversized satchel) mid-leap between roofs.
- *Caption:* "In the drowned city, the letter-runners never stopped."
- *Image 2:* Tam lands in a crouch. Behind her, unseen, a huge copper heron with glowing blue eyes watches from a chimney.
- *Sound effect:* THUD
- *Image 3:* Close-up: an envelope in looping handwriting reads "TAM." Her eyes are wide.
- *Thought:* "That's… my name."

**Page 2**
- *Image 1:* The heron spreads its metal wings; its shadow falls over Tam.
- *Sound effect:* KRRRRSHHHNK (metal unfolding)
- *Image 2:* Tam spins, hand on her strap, terrified.
- *Shout (Tam):* "WHO SENT THIS?!"
- *Image 3:* The heron tilts its head.
- *Wavy speech (heron):* "The one who waits at the top of the tower."
- *Image 4:* Tam looks at a distant black tower against the sunset.
- *Whisper (Tam):* "nobody comes back from the tower."

**Page 3**
- *Image 1:* The heron lowers its neck, offering a ride.
- *Speech (heron):* "Then you will be the first."
- *Image 2 (wordless):* Tam's hand hovers above the copper neck; her other hand grips the letter.
- *Image 3 (splash):* The heron launches with Tam aboard, wings wide against a red sky.
- *Sound effect (huge):* WHOOOOM
- *Caption:* "She did not look back at the city."

### Performance

*(low, steady)* In the drowned city, the letter-runners never stopped.

The water came up years ago and never went down, so now the streets are canals and the rooftops are roads, stitched together with rope bridges that sway every time the wind remembers them. *(slight lift)* And across one of those gaps, satchel flying, comes Tam.

*(quicker)* She's thirteen, and the bag is almost as big as she is. She clears the gap with nothing to spare and hits the next roof. THUD. She drops into a crouch, one hand flat on the warm tiles.

*(slower, quieter)* She doesn't see the chimney behind her, or what's perched on it: a heron made of copper, taller than a door, its eyes glowing a soft electric blue. It's watching her.

Tam is already flipping through the evening's letters. Bakery, harbormaster, bakery, *(stop)* and then one envelope in looping, careful handwriting. Three letters on the front.

*(inner voice, close)* That's… my name.

*(long pause)*

*(low, heavy)* Behind her, the chimney groans. KRRRRSHHHNK. Copper plates slide over one another, and a pair of metal wings opens wider than the rooftop. A long shadow pours over Tam and swallows the letter in her hands.

*(fast)* She spins, one fist locked on her strap. *(full voice, raw)* "WHO SENT THIS?!"

*(heron: deep, echoing, unsteady like a bell still ringing)* The heron tilts its head, gears clicking in its neck. "The one who waits at the top of the tower."

Tam turns. Far past the last rooftops, black against the dying sun, the tower stands like a splinter driven into the sky.

*(whisper)* "nobody comes back from the tower."

*(pause)*

*(soft)* The heron lowers its long copper neck until it's level with her knees. An offer. *(heron, calm and certain)* "Then you will be the first."

*(very slow)* Tam's hand rises and hangs there, an inch above cold metal. Her other hand is still crushing the letter. The wind picks up. The rope bridges creak. *(two-beat silence)* She climbs on.

*(build, rising)* The heron's legs bunch, and it throws itself off the edge of the roof. WHOOOOM. *(open up, big)* The wings catch the air all at once, and the whole red sky is under them, the rooftops dropping away, the canals turning to silver threads.

*(quiet, final)* She did not look back at the city.

*(long silence)*

## Performance checklist

**Before drafting**
- [ ] Arc mapped: setup, rising, climax, resolution
- [ ] Protected reveals and callbacks marked
- [ ] Every speaker has a label, voice settings and pronunciation
- [ ] The peak moment is chosen for the loudest voice or longest pause
- [ ] Reading format confirmed

**Every section**
- [ ] The one or two meaningful visual details identified
- [ ] What changed since the last image noted
- [ ] Gaps bridged with motion, sound or time, never layout
- [ ] At most one sensory addition, consistent with the art
- [ ] Speed matched to the moment
- [ ] Ends on its most open beat, with a held pause at the scroll break or page turn

**Voices**
- [ ] Each speaker recognizable without a tag
- [ ] No voice slips
- [ ] Labels varied, and names bridged once when revealed

**Weaving check**
- [ ] Did every bubble, caption and sound effect become part of the story instead of being read out separately?
- [ ] Shouts loud, whispers quiet, thoughts as inner voice, never announced
- [ ] Captions blended into the narrator's voice
- [ ] Sound effects happen in the scene; non-English lettering performed as sound
- [ ] Every page-text line verbatim, or omitted if unresolved

**Never**
- [ ] Layout words ("panel," "bubble," "the scene shifts")
- [ ] Contradicting the art, invented plot, changed or paraphrased page text
- [ ] Questions to the audience, asides, meta jokes
- [ ] Spoiled reveals, names the pages didn't give, unverified lines treated as confirmed
- [ ] Credits, watermarks or promo panels narrated

**Ending**
- [ ] Slowed down for the final section
- [ ] Ends on an image or line, then silence
